How Frequency Analysis Works

Substitution ciphers like Caesar shifts preserve the statistical distribution of letters. The most common letter in English is 'E' (~12.7%), followed by 'T' (~9.1%), 'A' (~8.2%), and so on. Even when encrypted, these frequency patterns remain constant.

The algorithm:

1. Count letter frequencies in the ciphertext.

2. Try all 26 possible offsets (Caesar cipher shifts).

3. For each candidate plaintext, compute a χ² (chi-squared) score comparing observed frequencies to known English frequencies:

χ² = Σ [(observed - expected)² / expected]

4. Lower χ² = better match. The candidate with the lowest score is most likely the correct plaintext.

Why this works: Letter distribution is robust under substitution. Your message's pattern of common/rare letters doesn't change when you encrypt it—only which letters are used. Frequency analysis exploits this immutable statistical signature.

Hint power: If you know one word in the plaintext, the algorithm can use that to boost the correct candidate's score, making the answer obvious even if statistics alone were ambiguous.
