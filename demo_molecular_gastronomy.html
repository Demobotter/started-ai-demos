🔮 Spherification

Sphere Strength: 45 g
Formation Temp: ~40°C
Membrane Type: Hydrogel
Gel Formation: 25%
💡 Fun Fact
Sodium alginate (from brown seaweed) forms spheres when Ca²⁺ ions cross-link its polymer chains, creating instant gel membranes. Used in high-end restaurants for "caviar" pearls.

🌊 Emulsification

Droplet Size: 12 µm
Stability: Stable
Viscosity: Medium
Hours Stable: 8
💡 Fun Fact
Lecithin's phospholipid molecules have one end that loves water and one that loves oil—they arrange around tiny oil droplets and hold them apart, making mayonnaise possible.

❄️ Gelification

Gel Strength (Bloom): 85 g
Gelation Temp: 40°C
State: Liquid
Texture: Firm
💡 Fun Fact
Agar is a polysaccharide from red seaweed that forms 3D networks, gelling at 40°C. Carrageenan forms different types (kappa, iota) with different gel properties—you can make firm gels or soft foams from the same base.

☁️ Foam Formation

Bubble Size: 120 µm
Air Volume: 35%
Density Reduction: 65%
Stability: 5 min
💡 Fun Fact
Lecithin stabilizes foam by forming a layer around air bubbles. The more you whip, the smaller and more numerous the bubbles—but too much breaks the structure. Chefs call this "engineering texture."

🌡️ Sous-Vide Precision

Core Temp: 55°C
Doneness: Rare
Protein Denaturation: 0%
Moisture Retention: 98%
💡 Fun Fact
Egg proteins denature at 63°C (coagulate), beef myosin at 63–75°C. Sous-vide holds meat exactly at 55°C for rare and juicy, then a quick sear creates crust. Impossible to achieve in a pan—temperature control is the secret.
🧪 About Molecular Gastronomy

Molecular gastronomy isn't magic—it's applied chemistry. Chefs use polymer science (gels), colloid chemistry (emulsions), and protein denaturation (cooking curves) to transform familiar ingredients. Every technique here is based on real food science data from published research, culinary textbooks, and USDA guidelines. Understanding the chemistry makes you a better cook and reveals why certain techniques work.
